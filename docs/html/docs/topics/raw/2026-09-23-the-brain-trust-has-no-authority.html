"How Pixar Fosters Collective Creativity" — Ed Catmull Harvard Business Review, September 2008 (Reprint R0809D) https://hbr.org/2008/09/how-pixar-fosters-collective-creativity

THE ARTICLE'S CLAIMS, as pasted:

The premise. A studio head told Catmull his central problem was finding good ideas, not good people. Audiences Catmull asks split about 50/50 on this. He disagrees completely: the belief is rooted in a misconception of creativity that exaggerates the initial idea.

Track record at time of writing: Toy Story (1995, first computer-animated feature) plus eight more films through WALL·E. Never bought scripts or movie ideas from outside; all stories, worlds and characters created internally. Dozens of patents.

Shared beliefs: talent is rare; management's job is not to prevent risk but to build the capability to recover when failures occur; it must be safe to tell the truth; constantly challenge assumptions and search for the flaws that could destroy the culture.

What creativity is. A movie contains tens of thousands of ideas — every sentence, every line reading, character and set design, camera position, colour, lighting, pacing. Every member of a 200–250 person production group makes suggestions. The leaders' job is sorting a mass of ideas for the ones that fit a coherent whole. "Like an archaeological dig where you don't know what you're looking for."

Toy Story 2. Begun 1996 while the proven creative leaders (Lasseter, Andrew Stanton, Lee Unkrich, Joe Ranft) were on A Bug's Life. A new, untested creative team led it. Disney initially wanted a direct-to-video, lower cost and quality; Pixar realised two standards of quality in one studio was "bad for our souls" and Disney agreed to a theatrical release — but the leadership stayed the same, which was the problem. Story reels were not where they should have been and were not improving. When A Bug's Life finished, the A team took over with eight months left where eighteen would have been aggressive. They kept the original core concept; the problem was that the audience could predict Woody's choice, so there was no drama. They added "Jessie's story" and the song "When She Loved Me" — Jessie had been a girl's darling and was discarded when the girl grew up — which made Woody's choice real.

The two lessons: "If you give a good idea to a mediocre team, they will screw it up; if you give a mediocre idea to a great team, they will either fix it or throw it away and come up with something that works." And: there has to be one quality bar for every film. Catmull records the cost — "We asked our crew to work inhumane hours, and lots of people suffered repetitive stress injuries."

Power to the creatives. Creative vision comes from one or two people, not corporate executives or a development department. After Toy Story 2 the development department's mission changed: instead of coming up with ideas, it assembles small incubation teams to help directors refine their own ideas. During incubation you cannot judge teams by the material, which is rough — you assess whether the social dynamics are healthy and whether the team is solving problems. "Filmmaker led": director and producer as a partnership, operating within time, budget and people constraints. "Good artists understand the value of limits." Operating decisions left to the film's leaders; no second-guessing or micromanaging.

The brain trust. John Lasseter and eight directors (Andrew Stanton, Brad Bird, Pete Docter, Bob Peterson, Brenda Chapman, Lee Unkrich, Gary Rydstrom, Brad Lewis). Convened when a director and producer want help; a lively two-hour give-and-take on the work in progress. "There's no ego. Nobody pulls any punches to be polite." It works because participants trust and respect one another. CRUCIALLY: "there are no mandatory notes, and the brain trust has no authority. This dynamic is crucial. It liberates the trust members, so they can give their unvarnished expert opinions, and it liberates the director to seek help and fully consider the advice." And the natural experiment: "When we tried to export the brain trust model to our technical area, we found at first that it didn't work. Eventually, I realized why: We had given these other review groups some authority. As soon as we said, 'This is purely peers giving feedback to each other,' the dynamic changed, and the effectiveness of the review sessions dramatically improved."

The dailies. Derived from practices Lasseter saw at Disney (small senior group only) and at ILM, where Dennis Muren broadened participation to the whole special-effects crew. People show work in an incomplete state to the whole animation crew; the director decides but everyone may comment. Four benefits: once people get over the embarrassment of showing work in progress they become more creative; the director can communicate points to the entire crew at once; people learn from and inspire each other; and there are no surprises at the end — "People's overwhelming desire to make sure their work is 'good' before they show it to others increases the possibility that their finished version won't be what the director wants."

Technology + art. Walt Disney's technological firsts (sound, colour, compositing with live action, xerography). Lasseter's saying: "Technology inspires art, and art challenges the technology."

Pixar's operating principles:

  1. Everyone must have the freedom to communicate with anyone. The decision-making hierarchy and the communication structure are two different things. Managers must learn they don't always have to be first to know, and "it's OK to walk into a meeting and be surprised."
  2. It must be safe for everyone to offer ideas. Works in progress shown internally with staggered viewings so there are always fresh eyes; everyone in the company attends at some point; everyone invited to e-mail notes to the creative leads on what they liked and didn't and why.
  3. We must stay close to innovations happening in the academic community. Technical artists encouraged to publish and attend conferences. "Publishing may give away ideas, but it keeps us connected with the academic community. This connection is worth far more than any ideas we may have revealed." Also: Pixar University (training and cross-training, plus optional classes from screenwriting and sculpting to Pilates and yoga, mixing novices with world-class practitioners), and the building — Steve Jobs's brainchild — "structured to maximize inadvertent encounters", with a central atrium holding the cafeteria, meeting rooms, bathrooms and mailboxes.

Staying on the rails. Catmull on watching computer companies with the best engineers and management make "stunningly wrongheaded" decisions at the height of their powers. "If we are ever successful, will we be equally blind?" Many people at those failed companies were not very introspective. Postmortems: the first (after A Bug's Life) was successful, later ones varied. People learn from them but don't like doing them; leaders want to give kudos; everyone wants to move on; left alone people game the system to avoid the unpleasant. Techniques: vary the format (same format finds the same lessons); ask each group for the top five things they would do again and the top five they would not; and use lots of data — "Because we're a creative organization, people tend to assume that much of what we do can't be measured or analyzed. That's wrong." They track rates at which things happen, how often something is reworked, whether work was finished when sent on. Fresh blood: two syndromes — the well-known not-invented-here, and "the awe-of-the-institution syndrome (an issue with young new hires)", which is often overlooked. Catmull speaks at new-hire orientations about the mistakes Pixar has made, to persuade newcomers that "we haven't gotten it all figured out" and that they should question things that don't make sense to them.

Context: written two years after the 2006 Disney merger, during which Bob Iger asked Catmull, Lasseter and other Pixar managers to help revive Disney Animation Studios.

The card's own arguments, labelled in the body:

  1. That the brain trust's authority-free design is the most transferable finding in the essay, and that Catmull reports an actual natural experiment for it — the same model failed when exported WITH authority and worked when the authority was removed.
  2. That the dailies invert the usual incentive: the cost of being seen wrong is removed up front, which is what makes risk cheap, and the fourth benefit (no surprises at the end) is the one that actually saves money.
  3. That "awe of the institution" is the more interesting of the two fresh-blood syndromes because a successful organisation silences newcomers automatically, with nobody intending it.
  4. That Catmull records the price of the one-quality-bar culture — inhumane hours, repetitive strain injuries — and that retellings of this story almost always drop that sentence.
