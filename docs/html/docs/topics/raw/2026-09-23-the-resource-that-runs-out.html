"Collaborative Overload" — Rob Cross, Reb Rebele and Adam Grant Harvard Business Review, January–February 2016 issue https://hbr.org/2016/01/collaborative-overload

--- THE ARTICLE'S OWN CLAIMS AND FIGURES, as pasted ---

Scale of the problem

  • Time spent by managers and employees in collaborative activities has ballooned by 50% or more over the past two decades (authors' own data collection).
  • At many companies, time in meetings, on the phone and answering email hovers around 80%.
  • Research across more than 300 organizations: 20%–35% of value-added collaborations come from only 3%–5% of employees.
  • Ning Li (University of Iowa): a single "extra miler" can drive team performance more than all the other members combined.
  • Mark Bolino (University of Oklahoma): "escalating citizenship".
  • Leaders are typically surprised by at least half the names on a network analysis of their strongest collaborators, because requests come from other units, offices or companies.

The three collaborative resources

  • Informational: knowledge and skills; expertise that can be recorded and passed on.
  • Social: awareness, access and position in a network, used to help colleagues collaborate.
  • Personal: one's own time and energy.
  • Informational and social resources can be shared without depleting the collaborator's supply. Personal resources are finite.
  • "Unfortunately, personal resources are often the default demand when people want to collaborate." Instead of asking for informational or social resources — or searching existing repositories such as reports or knowledge libraries — people ask for hands-on assistance they may not even need. "An exchange that might have taken five minutes or less turns into a 30-minute calendar invite."

The case study (a blue-chip professional services firm)

  • "Vernell", the top collaborator: 95 connections from incoming requests, but only 18% of requesters said they needed more personal access to him.
  • "Sharon", second most connected: 89 people, but 40% wanted more of her time — "markedly different, and more dangerous".
  • Threshold: as the percentage of requesters seeking more access moves beyond about 25, it hinders the performance of both the individual and the group and becomes a strong predictor of voluntary turnover.
  • Exhibit "In Demand, Yet Disengaged" (business-unit line leaders across 20 organizations): those seen as the best sources of information and in highest demand have the lowest engagement and career satisfaction scores. They either leave — taking knowledge and network with them — or stay and spread growing apathy.

Redistributing the work — three levers

  1. Encourage behavioral change. Show overburdened helpers how to filter and prioritize; give them permission to say no or allocate half the time requested; encourage introductions when the request doesn't draw on their unique contribution. Basecamp's notification "snooze button". At one Fortune 500 technology company, 60% wanted to spend less time on ad hoc collaboration requests while 40% wanted to spend more time training, coaching and mentoring; shifting contributions to those activities reduced stress and disengagement. Help seekers must change too: Dropbox eliminated all recurring meetings for two weeks, forcing reassessment; Rebecca Hinds and Bob Sutton (Stanford) found meetings got shorter and more productive even as headquarters headcount tripled over two years. Also encourage courageous action on decisions people should be making themselves.
  2. Leverage technology and physical space. Slack and Salesforce's Chatter for open threads; Syndio and VoloMetrix (acquired by Microsoft) for network assessment. A study led by Stine Grodal (Boston University) documented the detrimental effects of team meetings and emails on the development and maintenance of productive helping relationships; colocate highly interdependent employees for brief impromptu face-to-face exchange.
  3. Consider structural changes. Shift decision rights to more appropriate people (support staff or lower-level managers approving small capital expenditures, travel, some HR activities). Create a buffer: many hospitals assign each unit a nurse preceptor with no patient care responsibilities, available to respond to requests as they emerge — research by Adam Grant with David Hofmann and Zhike Lei found fewer bottlenecks and quicker connections to the right experts. Designate "utility players", possibly rotating the role.

Rewarding effective collaboration

  • Typically only about 50% overlap between top collaborative contributors and top performers.
  • Roughly 20% of organizational "stars" don't help — they hit their numbers but don't amplify colleagues.
  • Steve Kerr (former Goldman Sachs and GE chief learning officer): leaders hope for A (collaboration) while rewarding B (individual achievement).
  • Sports analogy: teams track assists, not just goals. Use network analysis, peer recognition and value-added performance metrics.
  • A life sciences company used these tools during a multibillion-dollar acquisition to find influential employees with broad impact but no formal authority, and distribute retention bonuses more fairly, instead of the consultancy's recommendation of leader retention bonuses.
  • At one investment bank, only those rated strong collaborators are considered for the best promotions, bonuses and retention plans.
  • Corning names fellows — a job and a lab for life — partly on being first author on a patent generating at least $100 million in revenue, and partly on having worked as a supporting author on colleagues' patents. (Disclosure in the article: Adam Grant has consulted for Corning.)

Why women bear more of the burden

  • Collaborative work falls disproportionately on women, who are stereotyped as communal and caring and expected to help with heavy workloads, mentor, recruit and attend optional meetings; evidence shows women experience greater emotional exhaustion than men.
  • 2013 Huffington Post poll: men were 36% more likely to share knowledge and expertise (an informational resource); women were 66% more likely to assist others in need (an action that costs more time and energy).
  • Experiment led by Madeline Heilman (NYU): a man who stayed late to help colleagues earned 14% higher ratings than a woman who did the same; when neither helped, the woman was rated 12% lower than the man.

Conclusion

  • "Collaboration is indeed the answer to many of today's most pressing business challenges. But more isn't always better." The authors suggest the time may have come to hire chief collaboration officers.

Reb Rebele, senior fellow for Wharton People Analytics and instructor at Melbourne Business School. Adam Grant, organizational psychologist at Wharton, author of Think Again and Hidden Potential.

Date matters and the card says so: this is from January–February 2016. Every number is a ten-year-old measurement of a trend the article describes as still climbing.

The card's own arguments, labelled as such in the body:

  1. That the three-resource taxonomy is really a rival/non-rival distinction, and that this makes it a reusable diagnostic rather than only an org-design observation.
  2. That the operational finding is the composition of demand, not its volume: 95 connections at 18% is safe and 89 at 40% is dangerous, so connection count is the wrong metric.
  3. That every tool which lowers the cost of asking raises the load on the same 3–5%, which the article's own mechanism predicts — and that agents are the next such tool, adding a class of requester that never gets tired of asking.
  4. That a knowledge repository is a machine for converting personal-resource requests into informational ones, which is what this repo is for, and that the article names the failure exactly: people ask a person instead of searching the repository.
  5. That Corning's criterion is the only form of "track assists" that changes behaviour, because it is a promotion rule rather than a value statement.
