"Between the spreadsheets — The management consultant's guide to love and sex"
By Alice Hines. The Economist, 1843 magazine, 20 February 2020. 25-minute read.
Illustrations by Noma Bar. No URL was given, so the item carries no --src-url.

THE ARTICLE'S CONTENT, as pasted (condensed; the full text is jay's paste):

Jacob, 32, works in finance building regulatory-compliance software; moved to New York from North
Carolina in 2013. Overwhelmed by the volume of dating-app matches, he built a spreadsheet, "How to
Choose a Goddess": 15 weighted attributes, scored per person, multiplied and summed. Great sex
weighted at roughly a third of great conversation, on the reasoning that people spend less time
doing it. "Status – friends admire her" 5, "Will be smart, dedicated mom" 9, "Emotional crush" 6.
He considered pairwise ranking and advanced statistics learned in a business-school pricing class,
then chose simpler: "A guesstimated number is better than not using numbers at all." To decide when
to stop dating he devised a spin on the Secretary Problem (classic solution: ~37%). One category
weighted 15 was entirely speculative — his estimate of how much his dates admired him. Hines notes
he was not rating looks; the scores measured compatibility.

The trend: partners not soulmates, deal-breakers, consent-before-emotional-labour memes. Carolyn
Yates (Los Angeles, polyamorous, queer, they/them): romance is "figuring out in an efficient manner
what works between people, just as much as being swept away in feelings"; keeps copious planner
notes; it feels like work and makes them more considerate. A polycule near Chicago used Excel to
plan an orgy — pronouns, likes, dislikes, boundaries, hard stops, aspirations, STD status,
preferences — which made it more "comfortable". A New Zealand couple ran Agile on their marriage:
monthly retrospectives, action points, month-long sprints.

History: marriage was an economic transaction and in places still is; "romantic" enters usage in
medieval chivalric tales; romance was injected into marriage only at the Industrial Revolution,
when wage labour moved work outside the home and the domestic sphere was annexed for romance. China
only the past 40 years; India's arranged marriages still the norm. In America in 2017, about 65% of
same-sex and 39% of heterosexual couples met on apps. A commenter on Jacob's blog: "Look in their
eyes. Sing songs together. Dance."

Hines's thesis sentence: people who use spreadsheets differ from the majority "only in their
conscious embrace of a fundamental truth – that relationships are transactions, and the work
involved in creating and maintaining them is a form of labour." Her interviewees had little in
common except that they ended up wondering whether more than one relationship was optimal.

Context: US marriage rates down 8% in ten years; home ownership out of reach; rent splits better
several ways; raising kids takes more than two people. About 5% of American relationships are
consensually non-monogamous (Chapman University / University of Michigan, 2014); one-fifth of
Americans report CNM at some point (Journal of Sex and Marital Therapy, 2017), higher among LGBTQ
and otherwise constant across age, education, income, religion, region, politics and race.

Jacob's story: an MBA classmate at UNC undressed; he fled, having previously reasoned himself out
of cheating. He was two years into a long-distance relationship with "Natasha" (pseudonym; she
declined to comment), a Russian on track to be a hedge-fund quant, met in Israel. He knew he loved
her when they stayed up solving probability problems in their underwear. He proposed an open
relationship; she had been about to ask the same. They moved to New York together.

Free-love history: Oneida (upstate New York, 1848) held "complex marriage" divinely ordained;
councils intervened when pairs grew too attached; children were taught to say "we" and "ours", never
"me" or "my". Oneida is now a homeware manufacturer; Kerista, a 1971 San Francisco polyamorous
commune, became a personal-computer distributor. The old critique of monogamy was that it was too
individualistic; today's is that it is insufficiently tailored to personal happiness. Jacob's blog:
"We spend longer than ever selecting the right partner and tie up ever more of our hopes, dreams and
identity in the partnership. Almost inevitably, the relationship fails to fulfil its promise."
Eleanor Wilkinson, a feminist academic, calls the present version a "watered-down, apolitical
vision" — optimising relationships as another facet of capitalist individualism.

Legal: group marriage is illegal in many countries and US employment discrimination on relationship
style is lawful, so some groups use an LLC or S-Corp to hold property jointly. Ora Prochovnick, a
San Francisco lawyer, says some clients shrink from the "business flavour" and others find it
spices things up.

Evin, Bunny, Katie, Madeline. Evin, a project manager, asked every partner the same day each week
for availability, then built a spreadsheet — partners as rows, days as columns, cells coloured
green/yellow/red — to schedule the desired number of visits. "It can certainly be seen as a
businesslike way to look at things, but the objective is quite the opposite. Scheduling is a
significant part of how I try to make sure partners feel respected." Evin and Madeline extend a
dominant/submissive dynamic through a productivity app: her daily to-do list (psychiatric
medication, cleaning her room) earns points and rewards. Her idea originally; she finds it more
helpful than paper planners. Their weekly check-in, the Six Questions, is answered in Google Docs
and then discussed. Sample: "What can I do this week to make you feel loved and appreciated?" Hines
calls it "a DIY version of couples therapy by way of a performance review". Madeline, previously
anxious in unfamiliar situations and inconsistent with medication on frazzling days, says running
her relationships procedurally solved most of that and helped her explore her sexuality.

Jealousy: Jacob treated it as a residual evolutionary by-product, made less sensible by DNA
testing, contraception and limitless options. "You could not make sharing people as seamless as
sharing rides." He found the rationality community — cognitive bias, hacking one's own mind, a
reputation for polyamory. Hannah Blume wrote about hacking herself to "become polyamorous over
(admittedly weak) natural monogamous inclinations", modifying her "factory settings", and declared
"I haven't suffered a hiccup of drama or a twinge of jealousy to speak of." Jacob distracted
himself with video games, habituated, and adopted don't-ask-don't-tell. He reframed anger: "she
doesn't respect my time" became "she probably got stuck somewhere and feels really bad".

THE SENTENCE THE CARD TURNS ON, verbatim from the article: "And he was also terrible at reading her
emotional signals. After all, he'd been doing everything he could to banish anything irksome from
his mind."

He spent his birthday with a Tinder date; Natasha was angry; "Why didn't you tell me ahead of
time?" / "You should have known." She broke up with him less than a year after they moved in,
weeks after signing another year's lease. She moved west to a monogamous relationship with someone
met while they were open. Jacob got over her by looking harshly at her behaviour and using dating
apps. Later he invited her back as a roommate, then her fiancé too; a kitchen-knife joke was taken
as a threat, the fiancé nearly called police, the couple moved out and broke off their engagement a
month later. Natasha no longer speaks to Jacob and has asked him to untag photographs.

Jacob does not blame rationality; he says he committed a cardinal sin of it by assuming others
thought as he did. "I don't think it ever crossed my mind that overriding my emotions and convincing
myself that Natasha and I were perfect for each other could be bad for me." Natasha had told him
emotional control was "impossible" — emotions can be repressed but never reconfigured, and feelings
in the raw are useful guides.

Stephanie, a biologist in the final year of a PhD, is the woman the spreadsheet advised him to
marry; high marks in "Can talk at my level about science, rationality, big issues" and "Openness".
The result surprised him — he had expected a woman with more immediate chemistry to win. She says:
"If I was entirely following my heart, I probably wouldn't be with him." When he told her about the
spreadsheet she smiled and asked a question about his algorithm design. They married in 2017; the
relationship has always been open apart from a few months at the start and a later stretch.
Stephanie: "everyone sort of does something like that…what are the pros and cons to being with this
person?"

Hines's own theory: spreadsheets work like OkCupid personality quizzes, psychoanalysis and
astrology — "All these systems provide structure to the murky depths of our feelings. They may seem
arbitrary but they help to make our contradictory and fluctuating desires comprehensible and
communicable."

Jacob now treats emotions as another data point: "If all you care about is forming true beliefs
about the world, listening to and understanding your emotions is a big part of it, because all you
have is your brain. And a big chunk of what your brain does is make you feel stuff." And: "Love is
still mysterious. Sex is mysterious. I'm just trying to shine a small light in the darkness."

The card's own arguments, labelled in the body:
 1. That the article contains its own falsification without quite stating it: the instrument Jacob
    used to suppress his discomfort was the same instrument he needed to detect Natasha's, and the
    article's own sentence about banishing anything irksome is the mechanism.
 2. That the highest-weighted category was the least measurable — his guess at how much dates
    admired him, weighted 15 — and that a weighted model concentrates error into whatever you
    weighted most, which is usually what you can least assess.
 3. That what the model does is not choose but commit: Stephanie's "if I was entirely following my
    heart I probably wouldn't be with him" is the evidence, and a stated model's value is that it
    can be examined and argued with, which a gut feeling cannot.
 4. That the practices which actually survived are the boring recurring ones — availability grids,
    the Six Questions, planner notes — and that none of them optimise anything; they are scheduled
    attention.
 5. That the historical frame defuses the genre: romance in marriage is an Industrial-Revolution
    artefact, so optimisation is a reversion rather than a novel corruption, which narrows the
    question to which model and what it costs to run.
